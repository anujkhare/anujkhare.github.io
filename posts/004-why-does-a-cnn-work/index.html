Why does a CNN work?

From a linear model to a ResNet on Imagenette, looking inside at each step.

A photo shows a man holding a large fish. A trained network says “tench”, and it is right. It has no notion of men or fish. It has 2.8 million numbers and 18 layers of arithmetic. When we split its score by position, most of the evidence sits on the fish.

Each position’s share of the winning score, on the most confident correct photo of each class. Most of the evidence lands on the object.

This post builds up to that network from the simplest image classifier on one dataset, and at each step looks inside to see why the next idea is needed. The aim is intuition, so most of the evidence is pictures of the model’s insides, not comparisons of architectures.

The ladder#

Everything runs on Imagenette: ten easy ImageNet classes, cropped to 160x160, with 9,469 training photos and 3,925 test photos. Every model got the same recipe: 200 epochs, flips, random resized crops, cutout, and AdamW with a cosine schedule.

Model Parameters Clean train Test
Linear 768,000 36.8% 35.5%
MLP, 256 hidden units 19.7M 63.9% 58.1%
Small CNN 368,842 99.3% 82.9%
ResNet-18 shape 2.8M 100% 87.4%

The biggest jump is the third row. Adding convolution gains 25 points and cuts the weights by more than 50 times. Everything after that, residual blocks and normalization and much more depth, gains 4.5 points for about eight times the weights.

One template per class#

A linear model learns one image per class and scores a photo by how well it matches. Its templates look like noise. A church has no single average appearance, and a template tied to fixed pixels can’t describe all the ways a church can fill the frame. It tops out at 35.5%, and it can’t even fit its training set under augmentation.

Hidden layers learn real patterns, once per place#

An MLP’s hidden units combine pixels into features, and it reaches 58.1%. Drawing a hidden unit’s weights as an image shows what it learned.

These are real patterns, but each one spans the whole frame and is fixed to one place.

Colour blobs, stripes and bars are all there. But a unit that detects a stripe here says nothing about the same stripe over there, which needs another unit. That’s where 19.7 million weights go.

We predicted that moving a photo would hurt the MLP’s accuracy. It didn’t: over moves of up to 20 pixels, its accuracy stays between 55% and 60%, because the augmentation had already shown it such moves. The damage shows up in its answers. A 5-pixel move changes 18% of the MLP’s predictions, against 6% for the small CNN and 3% for the ResNet. The MLP stays about as accurate by being right about different photos.

Convolution: one filter, every position#

A convolution takes a small grid of weights and slides it across the image. At each position it multiplies and adds, so the output is high where the patch matches. Two things follow. The weights are shared, so a stripe detector works everywhere. And the output keeps the input’s layout, which makes it a map: this feature, at these places. The final model looks like this:

stage 2, 2 blocks   40 x 40 x 64
stage 3, 2 blocks   20 x 20 x 128
stage 4, 2 blocks   10 x 10 x 256
average over positions       256
linear                        10

Code: resnet.py. The block is two 3x3 convolutions with GroupNorm, plus a skip.

What the layers see#

The trained first-layer filters are a disappointment. They’re smoother than the noise they started from, but they don’t read as pictures. The responses are much easier to understand than the weights.

Early maps still look like the photo. Late maps are coarse blobs that say what, and only roughly where.

The arches, the horn’s tubing and the outline of the fish are all clear in the early maps. By 10x10 the picture is gone, and what’s left is a statement of what the network found. Here are the same stages for your photo. Select a channel to enlarge it.

Late units can do this because their reach grows. A unit after the first convolution can see 7 pixels across. By the last stage it can see 219, more than the whole photo. The gradient of a central unit shows that it mostly uses the middle of that reach, as Luo et al. found.

A unit can reach the whole box, but it mostly depends on the pixels near its centre.

Going deep breaks things#

Stacking layers isn’t free. These curves come from untrained 56-layer networks, so they show what the architecture does before any learning.

GroupNorm steadies the signal and skips steady the gradient, and the model uses both.

With neither tool, activations fade about ten orders of magnitude by the last block, and gradients sit near 1e-10 throughout. GroupNorm alone holds activations steady, but gradients grow about 50 times on the way back to the first block. Skips alone keep gradients level but let activations grow 16 times. With both, each stays within a factor of about six.

Training agrees. On CIFAR-10, after 15 epochs, a plain 56-layer network reached 34% while the same depth with skips reached 78%.

Where is the evidence?#

The final network averages its 10x10 grid and applies one linear layer. So each class’s score is the mean of 100 per-position scores plus a bias, exactly. That’s the class activation map, and with this architecture it’s arithmetic and not an approximation. The first figure in this post is that map. It mostly lands on the object, with exceptions: the parachute’s evidence sits on the skydiver and the smoke trail, not the canopy.

Where it fails#

Chain saws and gas pumps are the weakest classes, at 78%. The most confused pair is gas pumps and cassette players, in both directions: 24 and 22 test photos.

Both classes are boxes covered in small panels, labels and readouts.

The evidence for the wrong class gathers around those panels. That’s a suggestion from a handful of maps, not a measured cause, but it’s a better explanation than the accuracy table gives. Open a mistake below to check its evidence for yourself.

The model is also sure of itself. It’s at 100% on clean training images, and its median top probability is 99.98% on correct test photos and still 78.5% on wrong ones.

What surprised us#

  • Accuracy hid the shift problem. The MLP’s flat accuracy curve looked like a refuted prediction, until we counted how many answers changed.
  • Gradients didn’t vanish with GroupNorm. Our notes said they faded toward the input. Plotted block by block, they grow instead.
  • Every pixel technically reaches every unit. GroupNorm’s statistics cover the whole image, so a test that checked the receptive field against gradients failed until normalization was removed.
  • Residual blocks aren’t small edits. On the trained model the residual path is about as large as the skip, and more than five times larger in the last block.
  • The test set had a hidden order. Our loader dropped the last partial batch, which on an unshuffled set removed one class. Shuffling at load fixed it.
  • Resuming needed a permission that saving didn’t. Checkpoints in a fresh bucket folder worked, but resuming needed read access to the bucket’s metadata. The small CNN paused at epoch 100 until that was granted.

Training ran on a Colab TPU (v5e1) at about 6.5 steps per second, limited by image augmentation, so 200 epochs took about 40 minutes.

Try it yourself#

Upload your own photo in the bar at the top of the post, and cover the part you think matters. Try something outside the ten classes, and see how sure it still is.
